Realism

Realism, alongside Liberalism and Constructivism, is one of the 3 "traditional IR theories". Fundamentally, it is concerned with power; which states have it, which do not, and how that influences state relations.

The Core Assumptions

Realism traditionally assumes that global politics is at its heart about power struggles between states. It believes this to be the case because for realists, the international system is one of anarchy. They argue that anarchy is the reality of the international system because there is no higher authority than the state. Consequently, states are said to first and foremost value survival and security as well as the pursuit of greater power. This is also called Power Politics. Though it is important to note that there is variation amongst realists and not all realist theories are the same.

Types of Realism

Classical Realism

Classical realism sees power politics as a consequence of human nature. Classical realists argue that there is egoistic and selfish tendency to human nature which then manifests itself in state behavior.

Morgenthau, 1962

"The social world is but a projection of human nature onto the collective plane"

Neorealism

Neorealism, also called structural realism, argues that state behavior is first and foremost influenced by the (anarchic) structure of the international system. Unlike classical realism, human nature is not the cause of conflict. Instead they argue the anarchy of the international system is what causes competition. There are varying perspectives on how states react to structural pressures.

Uncertainty

The uncertainty described in neorealism comes from the fact that, in anarchy, there is no way to know for certain that another state is being honest. They may tell you they want to be friendly and maintain good relations, whilst at the same time mobilizing to invade you. This is then said to lead states to assume the worst, making them pursue power as a matter of security and self preservation in the event of conflict.

Offensive Realism

The fundamental assumption of offensive neorealism is that, in an anarchic system, it is uncertainty of other states' intentions that push states to pursue more power for themselves. As such great powers pursue the greatest relative power possible. Offensive realists argue that great powers can only truly achieve regional hegemony as the greatest extent of their power.

Defensive Realism

Defensive realism conceptualizes states as pursuing security, not power. The argument is that excessive power accumulation makes other states perceive you as a threat. If you expand too much (territorially or otherwise) other states will seek to counter-balance this strength which would have the consequence of reducing security. Instead of pursuing power for power's sake, states pursue security by utilizing "balancing strategies". There is internal balancing, which builds capabilities: arms manufacturing, infrastructure development suited for military use, or more direct security facilities like fortifications and air defenses). There is external balancing in the form of building alliances with other states. Finally states practice restraint which keeps them from being subject to counter-balancing.

Neoclassical Realism

Neoclassical realism agrees with neorealism that anarchy matters, but they add another force: domestic factors. Domestic factors may include metrics like a state's capacity to act, the domestic politics and ruling ideology, leadership perceptions and decision-making. For neoclassical realists the dynamic works so that structural pressures provide boundaries on decision making whilst domestic factors shape the ultimate choice of the state.

Example: Nazi Germany

The Nazis did not expand due only to systemic pressure, but also due to the domestic ideology of the regime.
