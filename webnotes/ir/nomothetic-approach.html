Nomothetic Approach

Philosophy of Science introduces two approaches for describing the world: Nomothetic and Idiographic. These two approaches help us to conceptualize the difference between Natural Sciencesand Human Sciences as well as analyze the diversity of the Social Sciences. These approaches are possibly the two most important concepts for the course as they are routinely connected to other concepts

Overview

See Lecture 1 for more

The nomothetic approach aims to explain. Explanation is a term you will find in the nomothetic approach, far less so in the Idiographic Approach.

Im not reading all that

Nomothetic gives you "laws". Think of the laws of gravity or thermodynamics. Those are derived from this approach.

The nomothetic approach concerns itself with:

  • Identifying regularities in the world
  • Formulating generalizations and laws to describe those regularities
  • Deriving explanations of observed outcomes based on these generalizations and laws.

The above is copied almost exactly from the slides but it is how he words it in more than one lecture.

The nomothetic approach is typical practice for the natural sciences. That being said it does find uses in the human and social sciences.

Strengths

The nomothetic approach is useful for finding similarities and structures which underlie subjects of study which can seem diverse on the surface. The product of the nomothetic approach is broad "general" knowledge

Weaknesses

The nomothetic approach fails to represent the uniqueness of a given case. In this way it can be said to simplify complex ideas (which in some contexts also serves as a strength). Critics of the method argue it is reductive.

The Nomothetic Approach in the Natural Sciences

Case Study: Physics

See Lecture 3 for more

Physics has identified "laws of nature". These laws identify certain characteristics of the universes function. In the Natural Sciences (like physics), laws are mathematical equations. These laws are universal. This means they do not only apply only in certain cases, but in every case across the universe. This is often the case in the natural sciences.

The Inverse-Square law

If the equation above makes no sense to you don't worry it is not important and just might help those who do understand it. The important thing is that you understand this equation is used to determine the gravitational force (F) between two masses (m1 & m2). This equation might be used to determine the gravitational force between the sun and the earth. Without knowing this law we would not know that the gravity effecting objects can be reliably calculated. These are the type of laws the natural sciences are concerned with.

The Law of Radioactive Decay

Again don't stress the equation if it is not your cup of tea. Understand though that it is used to reliably determine how quickly an element decays (little pieces breaking off) over time (t) based on a known constant (the rate of decay, represented by λ). This equation, as with the previous, represents functions of the universe, hence the term "universal" to say they apply in every case (as far as we have observed, more on that caveat in Falsificationism).

Einstein's Mass–Energy Equivalence

If the examples from the slides didn't quite click I will offer my own example. You might be familiar with Einstein's mass–energy equivalence, it is fairly iconic and simple.
A brief (massively oversimplified) explanation is that the energy of a particle (E) is equivalent to the mass of that particle (m), multiplied by the speed of light squared (c2).

A practical use for this in terms you may understand is nuclear weapons. In a nuclear weapon there is a mass of an element. If we wanted to determine the energy released in that explosion we would be able to calculate that using this law of mass-energy equivalence because nuclear bombs function by turning the mass to energy.

What to take away

From these examples you should take away that these laws of nature are used for not only explaining how the universe works, but also for achieving practical effects. This is what is meant by "applying" a law.

Nomothetic Approach in the Human and Social Sciences

See Lecture 3 for more

The nomothetic approach is also used in the Social Sciences as well as Human Sciences. Laws in these sciences are slightly different from the laws of nature. They are not typically represented by equations but that is not to say they are never represented by equations (remember the GPE midterm for example). The nomothetic approach in these sciences assume that "human affairs exhibit significant regularities".

Examples

Here I will use just two examples, both from the slides. He offers 4 in the slides so if these are no good for you start from page 9 in Lecture 3. I will start with a simple one before moving on to a complex one, after which I'll offer a critical approach which will answer the obvious questions that arise.

IR example: Democratic Peace Theory

Democratic peace theory argues that democratic states do not fight wars against one another. This is not quantifiable into a formula like we saw with physics. Instead it is a more nuanced relationship which is dependent on the definition of democracy and war.

Gravity Model of Trade

I will preface by saying skip this example if you have zero interest in economics and/or physics because it might be too much and not necessary. That being said it may be interesting for some to see a case where a social science turns a law into a formula.

There is a subsection of economics which attempts to take the physical laws of nature and morph them into economic laws. I'll ask you to recall The Inverse-Square law from above. Economics took that same structure and applied it to international trade:

In this equation V is the volume of trade between two countries (It replaces F from the inverse-square law) m1 and m2 are the economic sizes of those countries (just as they represent object mass in physics), d is the geographical distance between those countries, G serves as a proportionality constant. This similarity has been used to suggest a certain relationship between physics and economics.

Put simply, this equation represents the fact that trade volume decreases as distance between trading partners grow. This relationship has in fact been observed in the real world. Where it differs however is it's predictive ability. Unlike the natural sciences there is no way to calculate any of these variables precisely. If you know all of the real-world numbers to fill in for the variables on the right side of the equation, V would not perfectly match what is observed like it would be with determining F in the inverse-square law.

Despite its lack of precise accuracy, the value in putting this into a mathematic equation is how it allows us to model economics. If you wanted to know whether to trade with a larger country that is farther from you or a smaller country that is closer to you, you can roughly make a prediction using this formula for which option would be more beneficial. Such considerations are absent from the Heckscher–Ohlin model of trade which is concerned only with production efficiency, not the efficiency of transport from producer to consumer.

The Issue with the Nomothetic Approach in these examples

You might be saying "but democratic states have fought one another". This problem arises because of that dependence on the definition of democracy and war that I mentioned. In 1969 El Salvador and Honduras fought a war against one another. Both states had held elections, but in practice the military held most of the power. Those looking to defend the theory argue simply that these states are not democracies. Whilst that may be a valid argument it brings to light the criticisms directed towards the use of the nomothetic approach in the human and social sciences: they are too complex and nuanced to be simplified into laws.

These laws are based on the assumption that human affairs, no matter the cultural or situational factors, have certain patterns and regularities which emerge.

The Takeaway

You should take away from this the fact that there is a discourse amongst scholars over the use of this approach in these fields. There is no "right" answer on whether or not they should be used.

You might notice for example that it is labeled "Democratic Peace Theory" and not "The Democratic Peace Law". This embodies the difference quite well. Democratic Peace Law sounds far less disputable than a theory.
