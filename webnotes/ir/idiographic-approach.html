Idiographic Approach

Philosophy of Science introduces two approaches for describing the world: Nomothetic and Idiographic. These two approaches help us to conceptualize the difference between Natural Sciencesand Human Sciences as well as analyze the diversity of the Social Sciences. These approaches are possibly the two most important concepts for the course as they are routinely connected to other concepts.

Overview

See Lecture 1 for more

The Idiographic approach concerns itself with understanding. This is a key factor which differentiates it from the Nomothetic Approach, which aims to explain. The idiographic approach is typical of the Human Sciences.

Strengths

The strength of the idiographic approach is in identifying differences between cases which might otherwise appear similar. It brings to light the details and context-specific knowledge for an object of study.

Weaknesses

The weakness of the idiographic approach is that it may not account for laws, structures, or constraints which influence an outcome. Furthermore, because it focuses on a single case, findings can not be generalized. In this way it may believe an observation to be unique when it is in fact common.

Examples

Freud and Little Hans

Sigmund Freud studied a 5 year old's fear of horses in 1909. In doing so he accumulated ~150 pages of clinical notes in which he delved into extraordinary detail about a single child's psychology. Freud did not aim to use this study to state "all children who are scared of horses behave like Little Hans". He did not want to formulate a law or a theory like a Nomothetic Approach would result in. His approach was solely interested in Little Hans, his case, his life, his fear. In doing so he was able to deeply understand a specific instance of horse phobia.

The strength of a study like this is that Freud may have been able to offer the boy or his parents advice for tackling the fear that only a deep understanding of the boy's mind could give. Perhaps he identified a unique cause of the fear which could be specifically addressed.

But on the other hand, Freud may not have considered more general solutions. Exposure therapy, or "facing your fears", is common advice for overcoming them. Being so focused on the unique characteristics of Little Hans' fear, Freud may not have suggested such a widely applicable solution. That being said, if exposure therapy and other common solutions did not work, an idiographic approach becomes very important as the only way to discover a solution.
