Falsificationism

Falsificationism

Why do we need to know about falsificationism?

Although philosophers of science no longer consider it to be the last word, the complete and true explanation of how science works, it remains an insightful suggestion for how to answer the question. Many scientists and scholars outside the field of the Philosophy of Science see falsificationism as the answer to scientific practice. It is common in academia to hear colleagues say "Interesting theory. But is it falsifiable?" as if it is the be all end all for the proposal to overcome. To know about falsification helps you know how to respond to such questions.

Karl R Popper

Karl R. Popper was born in Vienna in 1902. At the end of 1920 he took a PhD in philosophy. He escaped rising political tensions by moving to New Zealand where he taught from 1937-1945. Post-war he became a professor at the London School of Economics. He described himself as having founded the school of "critical rationalism", his own label for Falsificationism .

Popper holds a set of interconnected concerns which became the basis of Falsificationism. His first question asks "what is a sound way to test scientific hypotheses?". We can not simply accept hypotheses at face value but of course test them. He then asks about the creativity of science and how models of scientific practice can account for it. He finally asks how science differs from non-science. He wished to eliminate uncertainty from science. It is important to keep this final point in mind when understanding his material.

Testing hypotheses

In science we need to test hypotheses. Hypotheses are attempts to make sense of the way a phenomena may occur, to provide explanations of observations. Quite often we have an oversupply of hypotheses, thus the need to test them for their validity. At the highest level hypotheses tend to be both incredibly general and very abstract. This of course makes it difficult to test them thoroughly. But the hypotheses do deliver certain conditions under which you should be able to observe certain outcomes.

The Strategy

Hypothesis H entails observational claim O. We can therefore check whether O is true or false and then draw an inference about the adequacy of H.

The Newtonian Model of the Solar System

Taking this model as H, we cannot test it in its entirety. But we can still test O. One such O may be a total solar eclipse, predictable using this Newtonian model. To test it we simply observe if a solar eclipse occurs when one is expected by the model.

The outcome of O must always be either true or false. Popper identifies an asymmetry between these two outcomes. While O may be true, it does not rule out some other force being the true explanation. He highlights that observing O to be true gives us no certainty that H is true. But for cases in which O is false it must be that H is false. If the mechanism posited by H expects O absolutely to be the case then a failure to observe O is an absolute disproval. It is the asymmetry of certainty that acts as the engine of his philosophy of science.

Popper calls for abandoning every attempt to confirm hypotheses, instead he calls for only refutation of hypotheses as it is all we can be certain of.

Popper's Rejections of Induction

When he rejected confirmation, Popper rejected induction. Any confirmation of hypotheses is induction, sampling empirical data and it holding true is then seen to be validating the hypotheses as a whole. Popper thought that Hume's Problem of Induction was unsolvable. He campaigned to stamp out Induction in scientific practice. In this sense Popper was more Humean than Hume himself. Hume was nuanced and accepted to some extent a pragmatic acceptance of the problem and practicing induction despite it, although with the caution that the problem necessitates.

Science in Popper's eyes

Scientists can attempt only to falsify hypotheses. Attempts to confirm hypotheses leave us with uncertainty which is not an acceptable result. There is thus two results of a hypothesis test:

  • Hypotheses is falsified
    • The scientific community must abandon the hypothesis at once
  • Hypothesis is not falsified
    • The hypothesis survives to the next test but receives no confirmation. The hypothesis is not credited with its own survival.
      This method of trial and error is an attractively undogmatic picture of science. This is the dynamic of science according to Popper.

But what does scientific knowledge then consist of in this framing? Popper would say that scientific knowledge is not a set of true or highly confirmed findings, for to him we have no way of identifying true findings. Furthermore, scientific progress is measured by the elimination of errors, the hypotheses ruled inadequate. It is not the accumulation of truth. In many ways this is a characterization of scientific practice in "negative" terms.

The Origin of Hypotheses

The picture that Popper has painted is one where we have a throughput of hypotheses, running tests attempting to refute hypotheses, but this presupposes the existence of hypotheses. He sees hypotheses as the product of scientists minds, creativity, and curiosity. They may come from dreams, observations, and random associations, but all of the above are seen as equally implausible as a reason to believe a hypothesis.

This constitutes the first half of all scientific practice. The context of discovery is the phase in which scientists are free to engage in conjecture, to hypotheses boundlessly. It is then followed up by the second half, the context of justification, the phase in which scientists test hypotheses. They engage in logic and rigor, assuring the objectivity of science and test the products of discovery.

Falsificationism's Influence

Falsificationism still has wide influence outside of philosophy of science. Some use falsifiability criterion as test of scientific status of disciplines.

Critical Evaluation

Refutations is far less straightforward than Popper assumed. While the Newtonian Model of the Solar System may predict the position of celestial bodies, it does so with a set of auxiliary assumptions. Popper's understanding of science does not account for this. In this way some valuable scientific hypotheses seem not to be falsifiable. Falsification may be less relevant to scientific practice than popper thought. He neglects possible ways of making rigorous confirmation, spending no time at all on it. The pragmatic warrant of induction mentioned in Lecture 3 or Bayesian confirmation theory. These allow for a more positive characterization of scientific practice. Even Popper at the end of his career acknowledged "corroboration", a weak form of confirmation.

The sole falsifiable hypotheses are along the lines of "A is a necessary or sufficient condition for B". But not all hypotheses are strictly speaking falsifiable by one or a few instances. To say "A is statistically correlated with B" for example. Therefore, criterion of falsifiability may rule out example of good science.
